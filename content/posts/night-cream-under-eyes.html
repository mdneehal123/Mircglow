Can you use night cream under your eyes? Sometimes. A plain, fragrance-free night cream is usually fine on the under-eye area if you use a tiny amount on the bone below the eye. Creams with strong actives such as kojic acid, retinol, vitamin C or exfoliating acids need more care, and some are better kept away from the eye area completely.

It depends less on the word "night cream" and more on what is inside the jar and how close to the eye you apply it. Here is how to decide.

Why the under-eye area is different

The skin under your eyes is among the thinnest on your body. It has very few oil glands, it moves every time you blink or smile, and products can easily travel into the eye as you sleep. That is why it can feel dry before the rest of your face does, and why it reacts faster to strong ingredients.

Rich creams can also cause problems here. A thick layer can lead to tiny, hard white bumps called milia, and some people wake up with puffier eyes when heavy cream sits on the lids overnight.

How to apply night cream under your eyes

  1. Take a rice-grain amount for both eyes. That is much less than people expect.
  2. Use your ring finger. It presses more gently than your index finger.
  3. Dot it along the orbital bone, the bony ridge you can feel below the eye, starting from the outer corner.
  4. Tap, do not rub. Pat lightly until the cream spreads in a thin layer.
  5. Stay a little below the lash line. The cream will spread upward slightly on its own.
  6. Apply 20 to 30 minutes before bed, so it settles before your face touches the pillow.

Do you need a separate eye cream?

Not always. Eye creams are often gentler, thinner versions of face creams, sometimes with added ingredients like caffeine or peptides. They can be nice to have, but they are not essential.

  • You may not need one if your face night cream is plain, fragrance-free and does not sting near your eyes.
  • An eye cream may help if your face cream has strong actives, if your eyes water when cream gets close, or if you want a separate product for this area.

Dark circles and puffiness: what a cream can do

A moisturising cream can make the under-eye area feel softer and look smoother, because dryness makes fine lines more visible. That is a real, if modest, benefit.

It will not change dark circles caused by genetics, thin skin or the shadow of your bone structure. Puffiness from salty food, crying, allergies or poor sleep also comes and goes regardless of cream. What helps more:

  • Seven to eight hours of sleep, on a slightly raised pillow if you wake up puffy.
  • Less salt late in the evening.
  • A cool, damp cloth on the eyes for a few minutes in the morning.
  • Sunscreen around the eyes and sunglasses in strong sun.
  • Not rubbing your eyes, especially during allergy season or a dusty commute.

If dark circles or swelling appear suddenly, affect only one eye, or come with itching or pain, see a doctor.

Signs a cream does not suit your eye area

  • Stinging or burning that lasts more than a few seconds.
  • Watery or red eyes in the morning.
  • Dry, flaky patches under the eye.
  • Tiny white bumps that do not go away.

If you notice any of these, stop using that cream near your eyes. If cream gets into your eye, rinse with plenty of clean, cool water, and see a doctor if irritation does not settle. Short answers

Can you use night cream under your eyes? A plain, fragrance-free night cream is usually fine on the orbital bone. Be careful with creams that contain strong actives.

Can I use a kojic acid night cream under my eyes? Use it only on the outer eye area and bone, two or three nights a week at first, and stop if it stings.

How much night cream should I use under the eyes? A rice-grain amount for both eyes is enough.

Why do I get white bumps under my eyes from night cream? These are often milia, linked to heavy creams. Use a lighter cream or a thinner layer.

Will night cream help dark circles? It can help the area feel softer and look smoother, but it will not change dark circles caused by genetics or bone structure.

This is general skincare information, not medical advice. Patch test any new product, and see a dermatologist if you have a skin condition.
