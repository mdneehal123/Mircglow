Skin care for nurses comes down to three things: protecting hands that are washed and sanitised dozens of times a shift, keeping the face comfortable under masks and AC for long hours, and a short night routine that still happens when you are exhausted. A gentle cleanser, a plain moisturiser, sunscreen for the commute and a thick hand cream after duty do most of the work, and your hospital's infection-control rules always come first.

This guide is written for nurses, nursing students and other ward staff in Indian hospitals: 8 to 12 hour duties, rotating shifts, crowded buses or two-wheeler commutes, and very little time for yourself.

Hands first: the biggest problem for most nurses

Hand hygiene protects patients and you, so it is not optional. The goal is to keep the skin on your hands strong enough to cope with it. Damaged, cracked skin is harder to keep clean, so caring for it is part of good practice, not vanity.

  1. Follow your hospital's hand-hygiene and nail rules exactly: short nails, no rings or polish where they are not allowed, and only hand creams your infection-control team approves on duty.
  2. Dry hands fully after washing, especially between fingers, before putting on gloves.
  3. Use lukewarm water, not hot, when you wash; hot water dries skin faster.
  4. Moisturise in breaks if your ward allows it, using a fragrance-free hand cream.
  5. After duty and at bedtime, apply a thick layer of a rich cream or petroleum jelly to hands and cuticles. Cotton gloves over it for an hour (or overnight) help on very dry days.

If your hands stay red, itchy, cracked or weepy for more than a week or two, report it to your staff health or occupational health team and see a dermatologist. Reactions to gloves or hand products need proper diagnosis; do not keep working through them with home remedies.

Face care under masks and AC

Long hours in surgical masks or N95s are hard on the nose and cheeks. A thin layer of a light moisturiser 15 to 20 minutes before you put the mask on helps reduce friction, but keep it light so the mask seal is not affected; follow your hospital's fit guidance for N95s.

  • Skip heavy makeup and foundation under the mask; it traps sweat.
  • When you take a break, let your face breathe and blot sweat with a clean tissue.
  • Carry a lip balm; AC wards dry lips quickly.
  • After duty, wash your face gently with a mild cleanser, then moisturise.

Your locker kit

Keep a small kit in your locker or bag so care happens even on chaotic days.

  • Fragrance-free hand cream (one your ward allows)
  • A thicker cream or petroleum jelly for after duty
  • Lip balm
  • Sunscreen SPF 30 or higher for the ride home on day shifts
  • A mild face wash in a small bottle, and a clean face towel or tissues
  • Spare cotton mask ear-savers or a clip if your hospital uses them

A 3-minute routine before you sleep, at any hour

Whether you finish at 2 pm, 9 pm or 8 am, do the same short routine before sleep so it becomes automatic.

Sun and commute

Many nurses travel in daylight at the start or end of a shift. Apply sunscreen to face, neck and the backs of the hands before you leave home on day shifts. On a two-wheeler, wear a helmet with a visor and full sleeves; our skincare for bike riders guide has more. Tan fades slowly over weeks with shade and sunscreen; no cream changes it overnight.

Common mistakes

  • Using scented lotions or home remedies (lemon, raw garlic, spirit) on cracked hands.
  • Starting several new active products at once when your skin is already stressed.
  • Skipping cleansing after duty because you are tired, then sleeping in sweat and sunscreen.
  • Scrubbing the face hard to remove mask grime.
  • Ignoring a hand rash and hoping it goes away during a busy rota.

When to see a doctor or dermatologist

See a dermatologist, or your hospital's staff health team, for hand eczema that cracks, bleeds or itches, a rash where gloves touch, painful or cystic acne, or any skin change that keeps spreading. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your doctor before using creams with active ingredients.

Short answers

What is the best skin care routine for nurses? A gentle cleanser, a light moisturiser before your shift, sunscreen for the commute and a cream on face and hands before sleep. Keep it short so you can do it after every duty.

How can nurses stop dry, cracked hands? Dry hands fully after washing, use lukewarm water, moisturise when your ward allows and apply a thick cream after duty and at night. See staff health if cracks or a rash persist.

Can nurses use hand cream on duty? Usually yes, but use only the products your hospital's infection-control team approves, since some creams can affect gloves or hand rubs.

How do I stop mask marks on my nose? Use a thin moisturiser before the mask, take mask breaks where allowed and cleanse gently afterwards. Our face mask guide has more tips.

Which night cream suits tired skin after a long shift? A simple, gentle cream suits most. A plain avocado and aloe vera cream is a calm choice; add active creams only one at a time.

This is general skincare information, not medical advice. Patch test any new product, and see a dermatologist if you have a skin condition.
