Room heater skin care comes down to three things: keep some moisture in the air, sit and sleep at a safe distance from the heater, and seal water into your skin with a cream after every wash. Heaters warm the room by heating the air, and warm indoor air in winter holds less moisture than your skin would like, so face, hands and shins start to feel tight, itchy and flaky within a few days of switching one on.

This guide is for the long North Indian winter (Delhi, Punjab, Haryana, UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, the hills) and for anyone who runs a heater, blower or angithi through December and January. It covers the room, your daily routine, the night routine and the warning signs that need a doctor.

Why a room heater dries out your skin

Winter air outside is already dry. When a heater warms that same air indoors, the air can hold more water than before, so it "pulls" water from the most available place: the top layer of your skin. Long hot baths, hard water and wool against the skin add to it. The result is the same whether you use an oil-filled radiator, a fan heater, a halogen heater or a blower, though air blown straight at your face usually feels the driest.

Set up the room first

  1. Keep a bowl or bucket of water in the room, not on the heater. It adds a little moisture back as the room warms. A humidifier does this better if you have one; clean it as the maker says.
  2. Do not point the heater at your face or bed. Angle it towards the middle of the room and keep at least an arm's length or more between you and the heater, following its safety manual.
  3. Use a timer or switch it off once the room is warm. A warm quilt keeps you comfortable through the night; the heater does not need to run till morning.
  4. Keep a little ventilation. Open a window slightly for a few minutes every few hours. Never sleep in a closed room with a coal angithi, sigri or any fire burning: the fumes can be deadly.
  5. Drink water through the day. People feel less thirsty in winter.

Daily routine for heater season

Morning

  • Wash the face with lukewarm water and a gentle, non-foaming or mild face wash. Hot water feels good in the cold but dries the skin further.
  • Pat dry and apply a moisturiser while the face is still a little damp.
  • Apply sunscreen if you step out. Winter sun is softer, but it still reaches your skin, and it matters more if your cream has kojic acid.

After a bath

  • Keep baths short and the water warm, not hot.
  • Apply body lotion or a little oil on damp arms, legs and heels before you dress.
  • Wear a cotton layer under woollens if wool makes you itch.

Night routine when the heater runs in the bedroom

Night is when heated air does the most damage, because you spend 7–8 hours in it without touching your skin. A cream at bedtime helps hold water in until morning.

  1. Wash off the day with a gentle face wash. If you wore makeup, see how to clean makeup off before bed.
  2. While the face is still slightly damp, smooth on a pea-sized amount of night cream (about 0.4–0.5 g) over face and neck.
  3. Press a thin extra layer on dry patches around the nose, mouth and eyebrows.
  4. Apply lip balm and hand cream last.

Oily, sensitive and acne-prone skin near a heater

  • Oily skin: do not skip moisturiser just because your forehead shines. Use a thin layer all over and a little more only where it feels tight. Our guide to oily skin in winter explains the balance.
  • Sensitive skin: keep the routine short, avoid scrubs and strong acids while the heater season lasts, and patch test anything new. See how to patch test a face cream.
  • Using retinol or acids: dry heated air can make them sting more. Drop to 2–3 nights a week and moisturise well on the other nights.

Mistakes that make heater dryness worse

  • Sitting right in front of the heater for hours with your shins or face close to it.
  • Very hot baths, twice a day.
  • Washing the face many times a day with a strong foaming face wash.
  • Applying cream on fully dry skin, an hour after washing.
  • Peeling or scrubbing flakes off instead of softening them with cream.
  • Sleeping with the heater blowing towards the bed all night.

When to see a doctor

See a dermatologist or doctor if itching keeps you awake, the skin cracks and bleeds, you see red, scaly or weeping patches, or a net-like brown or reddish pattern appears on the shins or thighs after sitting very close to a heater for weeks. That last pattern is a known heat-related skin change and needs a doctor, not a cream. Also get medical help straight away for headache, dizziness or nausea in a heated closed room.

Short answers

Is a room heater bad for skin? A heater is not harmful in itself, but it makes indoor air drier, which can leave skin tight and itchy. Keep water in the room, keep your distance and moisturise after every wash.

How do I protect my face from heater dryness? Wash with lukewarm water, apply cream on damp skin, keep the heater pointed away from your face and use a bowl of water or humidifier in the room.

Which cream is good for skin in a heater room? A cream (not a gel) suits heater dryness better. Dry skin can use a richer cream at night; oily skin can use a thin layer of a light cream.

Should I sleep with the room heater on? It is safer and kinder to your skin to warm the room first and then switch it off or use a timer. Never sleep with a coal angithi burning in a closed room.

Why do my legs itch in winter near the heater? Usually dry skin from heat, hot baths and wool. Lotion after bathing and a cotton layer under woollens help; see a doctor if a rash or pattern appears.

This is general skincare information, not medical advice. Patch test any new product, and see a dermatologist if you have a skin condition.
